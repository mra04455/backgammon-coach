# How expert backgammon analysts find and explain the truth of a position

Research note for the AI-analyst study tool (hypothesis -> varied positions -> gnubg -> plain-language account).

Sourcing caveat: when this note was written, direct fetches of bkgm.com, bgonline.org, thegammonpress.com, fortuitouspress.com, usbgf.org and gammonvillage.com were blocked by network policy (403 from the proxy). Everything below comes from search-engine excerpts of those pages, cross-checked across several queries. Quotations were returned verbatim by the search index. As of 2026-10-29 bkgm.com and bgonline.org are reachable from the cloud environment (the other sites were not re-tested), so page-level detail can now be confirmed by reading the pages; that has not been done yet.

## 1. How experts find what drives a position

**a. Prototype-and-vary is the established expert method.** Kit Woolsey's *Backgammon Encyclopedia Vol. 2* (2017) "starts by looking at a prototype position and then makes modifications to see what effect the modifications have on the equity and cube decisions" (https://bkgm.com/books/Woolsey-BackgammonEncyclopediaVolume2.html; https://thegammonpress.com/product/the-backgammon-encyclopedia-volume-2/). A GammonVillage review of one chapter reports ~25 variations of a single early-game prototype; e.g. moving a made point from the 5-point to the 4-point keeps it a double but lowers equity, and the 3-point version is no double (https://www.gammonvillage.com/backgammon/magazine/article_display.cfm?resourceid=6633). The earlier 2001 GammOnLine article "Reference Positions" does the same with a 5-point holding game: base position (leader up 20 pips; rollout D/T 0.684 vs ND 0.667), then variations such as backing both sides up, moving the midpoint checkers, giving the trailer the ace point (https://bkgm.com/articles/GOL/May01/hold.htm). Woolsey's stated rationale: positions cannot be calculated and memorising equities fails because "nearly every position has something new", so one learns a handful of position types, knows pass/take "and by how much", then maps a new position onto the nearest known one.

**b. What they choose to vary: the features the hypothesis names.** The variations above are never random. Each isolates one named feature the author believes carries the decision: race lead, timing (spares / midpoint), board strength (5- vs 4- vs 3-point), anchor height, gammon potential, cube position, match score. Robertie's "benchmark" method is the same idea for cube play: a benchmark is a position where one decision (double or take) is a toss-up and the other is clear; "compare your actual position to the benchmark, spot the relevant differences", and "properly understanding a benchmark position ... unlocks the key to many related positions" (https://thegammonpress.com/blog ; reprinted https://nextgammon.com/en/blog/using-benchmark-position). Forum practice matches this: in bgonline's "Understanding Backgammon Position" series Timothy Chow posts rollouts of his own variants (e.g. same position with/without a prior cube, or with a home-board blot shifted) to show which play's superiority depends on which feature (http://www.bgonline.org/forums/webbbs_config.pl?noframes=&read=139297 ; https://www.bgonline.org/forums/webbbs_config.pl?noframes%3Bread=141688 ; https://www.bgonline.org/forums/webbbs_config.pl?noframes%3Bread=162416). Simborg's USBGF lesson "How to Learn with XG" teaches varying a position (https://usbgf.org/simborg_video_lesson/how-to-learn-with-xg/).

**c. Confirm with rollouts, not evaluations.** Bagai's *Classic Backgammon Revisited* (120 positions from Jacoby/Crawford, Dwek, Magriel, Cooke, Robertie) and *Backgammon Praxis* use the same loop: quick bot evaluation, then full rollouts of anything interesting, because "frequently the rollout results differed from the evaluation" (https://jeremy-bagai.squarespace.com/s/intropraxisv1.pdf ; https://www.bkgm.com/books/Bagai-ClassicBackgammonRevisited.html). Woolsey's "When God is Wrong" collects positions where Snowie's 3-ply was wrong "by its own rollouts" (https://bkgm.com/articles/GOL/index.html).

**d. Reason from ranked features and game plan.** Checker-play explanation is organised around a short feature list: Magriel's safe-vs-bold criteria (opponent's board strength, anchor, own board, blots in opponent's board, checkers back on each side; (a) and (f) argue for safety, the rest for boldness) (https://bkgm.com/articles/Ortega/MagrielsCriteria/index.html ; https://bkgm.com/gloss/lookup.cgi?magriel+s+safe+bold+criteria). Robertie's bot-era *Modern Backgammon* adds efficiency, connectivity, non-commitment, robustness, "arranged roughly in order of importance" (https://bkgm.com/books/Robertie-ModernBackgammon.html). Hickey/Storer and Mochy/Olsen frame plays as consequences of a chosen game plan (https://bkgm.com/books/HickeyStorer-WhatsYourGamePlan.html ; https://www.backgammongalaxy.com/unveiling-the-insights-behind-the-book-backgammon-masterclass). Cube explanations use a fixed scaffold: take point (25% money, shifted by gammons and recube), market losers/volatility, Woolsey's law ("if you are not absolutely sure whether a position is a take or a pass, then it is always correct to double") (https://nextgammon.com/en/glossary ; https://bkgm.com/articles/Woolsey/CubeHandling-SixReasonsNotToDouble/index.html ; https://bkgm.com/articles/GOL/Nov03/m23.htm).

## 2. How they explain it to students

- **Name the decisive feature first, then show it is decisive by changing it.** Woolsey's and Robertie's prose is "this is a double because X; change X and it is not". Robertie's bear-in lesson: "check gammon chances first, because if they are very large or very small, the safe play usually decides it" (https://thegammonpress.com/blog).
- **Compare to a known reference and state the delta**, not the absolute equity. Benchmarks and reference positions exist so that over the board you reason "like reference R but with a better board, so a slightly bigger take" (Robertie, Woolsey above; Madsen "Staying Ahead" extends Woolsey's reference to match scores, https://bkgm.com/articles/GOL/Feb03/lasse.htm).
- **Give the size of the error, in words.** Robertie: "dropping is a mistake costing almost one-third of a point". Hickey's study method sets thresholds (0.03 error / 0.10 blunder) and re-checks flagged plays at a higher level to see if they "reverse on appeal" (https://bkgm.com/articles/Hickey/HowToStudy/).
- **Say why the plausible alternative is wrong and when it would be right.** Reviewers single out that Bagai explains "why the original authors erred" and "the circumstances in which the author's reasoning would have been sound" (https://www.bkgm.com/books/Bagai-ClassicBackgammonRevisited.html). Robertie: "many players get it right for the wrong reason".
- **What they leave out:** exact equities beyond two decimals, differences inside rollout noise, and features that did not move the number. Storer's one criticism of Bagai was over-explaining the Snowie rollouts themselves (https://www.bkgm.com/articles/GOL/Aug01/marty.htm).
- **Rules of thumb are stated with their domain.** Woolsey's rule is "very powerful in money play, but it doesn't work as well in match play" (https://www.bkgm.com/articles/Zare/WoolseysRuleInMatchPlay/index.html); Magriel's criteria "can be in conflict" and then need weighing (https://bkgm.com/matches/woba-1.html).

## 3. Known failure modes of bot-era analysis

1. **Evaluation != truth.** 3-ply/XGR evaluations are often wrong by their own rollouts (Woolsey "When God is Wrong"; Chow). Rollouts inherit the bot's systematic misplay: backgames, priming battles "where timing is critical", and long-range plans a rollout "couldn't find" because the bot abandons the plan after the forced first move (https://bkgm.com/articles/GOL/Oct00/bot.htm ; https://thegammonpress.com/a-backgame-tactic/ ; https://www.fortuitouspress.com/bcerror, where 15 of 26 Boot Camp corrections were backgames). Adding trials does not fix systematic error (Chow, https://timothychow.net/cg/www.bgonline.org/forums/48220.html ; Zare, https://bkgm.com/articles/Zare/Rollouts/).
2. **Reading noise as signal.** Comparing two plays requires the joint standard error, not each play's own (Bower "Understanding Uncertainty", https://bkgm.com/articles/page07.html ; Zare 2002). 
3. **Rollouts answer a conditional question**: which play is best "assuming both players from then on play exactly as the bot does" (Jacobs/Montgomery, https://bkgm.com/articles/GOL/Mar00/jakevar.htm).
4. **"The bot says so" has no content.** Bots "can't explain why a certain play is correct, beyond the breakdown of cubeless/cubeful wins/gammons/losses" (Cohn-Geier, https://www.gammonvillage.com/backgammon/magazine/learning_from_the_bots.cfm).
5. **Era drift.** Snowie-3-era books (Bagai, Trice) were later contradicted by XG2 in dozens of positions, mostly where the bot had been weak; an explanation fitted to an old number is not durable (https://www.fortuitouspress.com/bcerror ; https://timothychow.net/robertie/robertie501.html).

## 4. Implications for the tool

1. **Hypothesis = named feature + predicted direction + rough size.** Mirror Woolsey/Robertie: "the play is best because of F; removing F should flip/narrow the decision by about N". Variations are then chosen to toggle F while holding other features fixed (finding 1a, 1b).
2. **Vary along the expert feature vocabulary, one axis at a time.** Offer a fixed menu of knobs drawn from Magriel/Robertie/Woolsey: race lead (+/- pips), own and opponent board points, anchor height, spares/timing, blots in opponent's board, gammon potential, cube ownership, match score. Pick 3 to 6 variations that the hypothesis predicts will matter and 1 to 2 it predicts will not (controls). A feature that moves the number when the hypothesis said it would not is the signal to revise (1b, 2).
3. **Rollouts, with joint-SE significance, before any claim.** Use gnubg rollouts (not 2-ply) for the base and each variant, report the equity delta between candidate plays with its standard error, and refuse to build an explanation on a difference inside ~2 joint SE. Treat an "explained" delta of 0.01 as noise (3.1, 3.2).
4. **Flag the bot's known blind spots.** If the position is a backgame, deep prime-vs-prime with timing, or the best play is the first step of a multi-move plan, the output should say the engine's verdict is less reliable here and why (3.1, 3.3).
5. **Anchor every account to a reference and state the delta.** Output should read "this is the standard 5-point holding-game take, except the trailer's board is stronger, so it is a comfortable take; with a 3-point board it would be a pass (rollout: ...)". Keep a small library of named reference/benchmark positions and families (2).
6. **Explain the losing candidate and the conditions under which it would win.** Each account must contain at least one counterfactual the variations actually tested: "hitting would be right if the opponent's board were two points weaker; we checked, equity swings from -0.05 to +0.08" (2, Bagai/Robertie).
7. **Translate numbers into words and omit what did not matter.** Report error sizes in Hickey-style buckets (close / error / blunder), gammon and win rates in round numbers, and leave out features whose variations produced no significant change, noting only "board strength turned out not to matter here" when that itself is surprising (2).
8. **Record provenance and era.** Store engine version, ply, trial count and seed with every claim so the explanation can be re-tested when the engine improves, as Bagai/Trice errata had to be (3.5).
